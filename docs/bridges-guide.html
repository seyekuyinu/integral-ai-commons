Staying in charge of your AI

You do not need to be technical to read this. It is about keeping your judgment, your voice, and your community's say — while still getting the help.

Most advice about AI is either "it will take your job" or "here are nineteen prompts that will 10× your business." Both skip the part that actually matters: what using these tools every day does to you.

That is the question underneath everything here. Not what the tool can do. What it does to the person using it.

The human is not here to serve the tools. The tools are here to serve the human.

The one distinction to hold

There is work that has soul in it, and work that does not.

No soul — hand it over Formatting a document. Turning your notes into a first draft. Summarizing a long report. Building a spreadsheet. Researching what already exists. Cleaning up a list.

Soul — keep it Who to hire. What to say to someone who is hurting. Whether to take the money. What your organization is actually for. Which promise you are willing to make. What "doing right by this community" means here.

AI is genuinely good at the first list. It has no standing on the second — not because it is not smart enough, but because those answers come from knowing people, carrying history, and being accountable to someone. It has none of that. You do.

The trouble is that it will answer anyway, fluently and fast, and fluency reads like authority. Which is why the line has to be one you hold.

Five habits

1. Make it sound like you, or don't send it

If a message sounds like AI wrote it, the person reading it can tell, and something is lost that you will not get back. Before you send anything drafted by AI, read it out loud. If you would not say it that way, change it until you would.

Try: "Here are three things I've actually written. Match how I sound, not how you sound."

2. Ask for options, not answers

Whenever the decision is yours, change the question. "What should I do?" invites the tool over the line. "What are my options and what does each one cost me?" keeps it on the right side.

Try: "Give me two or three options with the honest downside of each. Don't recommend one yet."

3. Notice what you have stopped doing

This is the quiet one. Not that the tool does things badly — that it does them well enough that you stop practicing. Every few weeks, ask yourself: what could I do six months ago that I would now hesitate to do without help?

Some of that trade is fine. You do not need to keep your formatting skills sharp. But if it is your writing, your judgment, or your read on people, that is worth taking back.

4. Make it explain itself

If you cannot explain why you are doing something, you cannot defend it to your board, your staff, or the people you serve. "The AI said so" is not a reason.

Try: "Walk me through why. What would have to be true for this to be wrong?"

5. Ask who is not in the room

These tools learned from what was already written down, which means they carry the assumptions of whoever did the writing. When your work touches other people, ask the question out loud: who benefits from this, and who gets left out?

What to watch for

  • It sounds certain about something it cannot know. It does not know your people, your history, or last year's fallout. Confidence is not knowledge.
  • The output could have been written for anyone. If it is generic, it is not finished. Specific is the whole point.
  • You are checking with it before you decide. Getting a second opinion is fine. Needing permission is not.
  • It makes things up. Names, statistics, citations, quotes. Confidently. Check anything you would be embarrassed to get wrong.
  • You stopped reading before sending. The moment you send unread, it is no longer your message.

A fair test

At the end of a session with any AI tool, one question:

Did I come out of that more capable, or just further along?

Further along is fine sometimes. You had a deadline. But if the answer is always "further along," something is being traded that is harder to get back than time.

The goal is not to use AI less. The goal is that a year from now you are better at your work — not just faster at producing it.
